MRP-SMART

Estimating place-based and cross-sectional population outcomes from everyday transactions and behaviours

Author

Roger Beecham

Background / Summary

MRP-SMART is a collaboration between Financial (FINDS) and the Healthy and Sustainable Places (HASP) Data Services. The project will demonstrate how reliable, de-biased estimates of cross-sectional and neighbourhood-level population outcomes can be generated from non-representative smart data – individual observations describing everyday behaviours and transactions. It will do so via Multilevel Regression and Poststratification (MRP) (Gelman and Little 1997), a technique for small area estimation (SAE) little-used outside of political polling, but with obvious potential for addressing issues of representativeness in smart data research.

MRP-SMART will contribute:

  1. A collection of data products estimating the financial outcomes of populations living in GB neighbourhoods, communicated via modern uncertainty visualization (e.g. Franconeri et al. 2021) in a concrete, intuitive and policy-relevant way;

  2. Open source code products that build capacity across the smart data network, lowering the barrier of entry to MRP modelling;

  3. A flagship methodological paper, in Annals of the American Association of Geographers (AAAG), presenting a case and supporting framework for MRP in smart data research.

Why MRP for simulating public outcomes and behaviours?

In social research, samples from surveys are routinely used to estimate the characteristics of a population – levels of financial distress, dietary and physical activity, pro-environmental attitudes. With enough sample, we can directly estimate the prevalence of target outcomes at subnational (regional) level. If we want to look at smaller-scale geographies – in GB, the 40k LSOA / Data Zone neighbourhoods which tend to really discriminate social outcomes – direct estimation soon fails and we must turn to model-based approaches, or small area estimation (SAE).

In MRP, the outcome of interest is first modelled (MR) from survey microdata using a range of individual (respondent) demographics and group (area-level) context variables. Next, a ‘poststratification frame’ is constructed, whereby for every small-area unit, joint counts are derived from mainly Census data for the different demographics and area-level variables used in the model. At poststratification (P), predicted probabilities of the outcome are extracted from the model for each row of the postsratification frame. Multiplying these probabilities by the joint counts, we can estimate the extent that the target outcome occurs in the small-area.

The key advantage of MRP lies in its use of Bayesian hierarchical models. It is common in SAE that certain demographic combinations have little or no representation in the sample data. In these cases, the model uses information from related groups to make more accurate estimates, known as partial pooling. In a famous example, Gelman et al. (2016) used MRP on a succession of highly unrepresentative polls of Xbox customers, and documented how Xbox-derived forecasts of 2012 US presidential election were in-line with those of leading pollsters using large-scale and traditionally sampled surveys. Additionally, because of the fully-Bayesian approach, posterior distributions are generated for each prediction. This means that for any combination of spatial unit and demographic type interpretable indicators of the uncertainty around estimates can be reported.

Why MRP for FINDS?

FINDS works with full bank transaction records of over 5 million GB-based customers, and from here derives a set of ‘financial health’ indicators of those customers: people with persistently overdrawn accounts, living beyond their means, with low emergency resilience, as well as median weekly income. These indicators are presented to policy-makers and service providers via an interactive web-based dashboard, the Economic Wellbeing Explorer. This tool allows users to subset the data by geography, demographics and financial indicators. While 5 million customers is undoubtedly a large dataset, it is not a representative or probabilistic sample. If the ambition is to describe the financial outcomes of sub-populations – demographic and neighbourhood-level – then there is no guarantee that the customers recorded in that area accurately reflect the financial health of those sub-populations. Since direct estimation is used, the sample becomes surprisingly small, unreliable and potentially disclosive when subset by combinations of demographics and area-level variables. At present, data are therefore available at reasonably aggregated geographic spatial units and age bands.

As a principled, and empirically validated approach to correcting for bias in non-representative samples, MRP-SMART will be a significant addition to FINDS and the Economic Wellbeing Explorer tool. Estimates of ‘financial health’ outcomes could be generated for any combination of spatial unit and demographic combination, without risking disclosure and with interpretable uncertainty information expressed in the natural units of each outcome.

Why MRP for HASP and SDRUK?

MRP-SMART presents a real methodological opportunity for smart data research. The current standard for SAE, spatial microsimulation (SPM), relies on strong assumptions around individual sample data and their ability to discriminate target outcomes. MRP not only addresses these assumptions directly – and has been shown to produce more correct estimates of a ‘groundtruthed’ outcome than equivalent models generated via SPM (see Beecham and Clark 2025) – but is well-suited to the more prosaic characteristics of smart datasets. While a key difficulty with behavioural data is that they are demographically sparse, they do usually record at a fine-grained neighbourhood level the context in which individuals live. MRP is ideally practiced in situations where this individual geographic context is known, as it can be incorporated into modelled estimates without losing information via partial pooling (Hanretty 2019). For the sorts of natioanlly representative survey datasets on which MRP is typically conducted, this is not always the case.

MRP-SMART will contribute documented code repositories and open source packages that enable other smart data researchers to generate MRP-derived estimates on their own behavioural datasets. Built into the project is an exchange of learning between data scientists at FINDS and those at HASP, who will look to implement MRP on HASP’s SPM-derived place-based estimates on the Inclusive Economy and Consumer Vulnerability index (Adcock et al. 2020), as well as behavioural datasets describing mobility and physical activity behaviours that are currently being acquired by that data service.

Aims and objectives

The project delivers on SDR UK objectives insofar as it:

1. Addresses methodological challenges in smart data research

Bringing MRP outside of the political polling use case, and applying to behavioural rather than sampled survey data, is an exciting prospect. The project will develop and publish fully-documented and transferable code, using open-source packages for MRP implementation. It will enable researchers across the SDR UK network to generate population-level estimates on other behavioural datasets and in other themed domains. An immediate target will be the catalogue of in-production datasets at HASP, for example the loyalty-card derived Consumer Vulnerability Index.

2. Promotes trustworthy and responsible use of sensitive data

Each data point generated through the project will be a modelled value, with full uncertainty ranges, rather than a direct estimate from a sample. This means that the estimates are not disclosive of individuals. Coupled with our use of cutting-edge techniques for uncertainty representation (Franconeri et al. 2021), the project will demonstrate how uncertainty reasoning can be made intrinsic to smart data products.

3. Generates high-impact data products that enhance existing data assets

The datasets, estimating unknown financial outcomes at neighbourhood and cross-sectional level, will be a novel resource for analysts and policy-makers alike. The project will champion public policy engagement in these data products via data stories and visualization tools, built into FINDS’ existing Economic Wellbeing Explorer dashboard.

Expected outputs and deliverables

1. Data products and tool

Estimates of population-level financial outcomes, published across area-level and demographic dimensions and with interpretable uncertainty ranges for each point estimate.
These data products will be administered on the FINDS data catalogue and implemented in the Economic Wellbeing Explorer dashboard. Alongside the data products will be data stories that explain how MRP-based estimates are generated. These efforts will assist reliable interpretation of model outputs.

SDF data scientists have demonstrable track-record in dashboard design, and regularly update the Economic Wellbeing Explorer in-light of feedback from end-users. In addition to web-based app design (example), RB has extensive experience of communicating design stories to analysts and policy-makers: police analysts using statistical control techniques to study emerging crime patterns (Beecham et al. 2021) and transport planners using Bayesian estimates of road crash risk (Beecham and Lovelace 2023).

2. Code products and paper

Transferable code products for implementing and validating MRP estimates, with tailored guidance on constructing postratification frames.
For each published data product, we will document the model-building process in full, including decisions on variable selection and model parameterisation.

An in preparation demo for end-to-end MRP modelling has already been initiated as part of this proposal. The code ‘primer’ for MRP-SMART, developed collaboratively by RB and HASP data scientists, will be published via the Data:Code section of Environment & Planning B (Arribas-Bel et al. 2021) (EPB), a practice for which RB has recent track-record (Beecham et al. 2025).

3. Methodological framework paper

A flagship journal paper, in AAAG, presenting MRP for smart data research.
This work will build on RB’s recent GISRUK paper revisiting SAE in Geography and applied social science (Beecham and Clark 2025). Through empirical example, it will present a framework for SAE which involves building MRP models alongside standard approaches (such as SPM) to validate population estimates made in the absence of ground-truth data.

Expected impact

Impact on SDR UK Research Themes

1. Productivity and Prosperity

The enhanced FINDS data products will provide a unique profile of financial distress at the small-area neighbourhood level. These datasets will be a valuable resource for governments and service providers to design policy and implementations for monitoring and tackling inequality.

2. Sustainability

The code products could impact each of the SDR UK themes, as they enable researchers to generate MRP-derived estimates across domains. As part of a separate project, INFUZE, RB and HASP data scientists will apply learning from SMART-MRP on behavioural datasets collected from mobility service providers in order to simulate neighbourhood-level mobility behaviours and opinions. MRP-SMART will therefore inform service design around transitions to more sustainable mobility behaviours.

Impact on SDR UK Data Service Objectives

1. Safe access and responsible use of smart data for research

Non-disclosure of sensitive information is guaranteed in our MRP-derived data, since every data point is a modelled estimate. Techniques for representing estimate uncertainty, combined with explanatory data stories, will promote reliable and trustworthy interpretation by policy-makers and other dataset users. MRP-SMART will therefore be an exemplar for communicating smart data research outputs.

2. Building capability across the research community

The exchange of expertise is an important aspect of the project. Initially this will be between FINDS and HASP data scientists involved. Wider community impact will be effected via our open source and transferable code products – to be published in the Data:Code section of EPB. Through these efforts, the project will lower the barrier to entry to MRP across the SDR UK network.

3. Ground-breaking methods in smart data research

MRP provides a methodological framework for addressing issues of bias and representation when using smart data for social research. The project therefore hits the core of SDR UK’s mission and has the potential to provoke a new sub-field within smart data research – the ambition of our flagship methods paper (in AAAG).

Total cost and justification of resources

While funds will be delivered within the 2025/6 financial year, substantive work on the project will run over 12 months, starting October 2025 and ending September 2026. This activity is organised into four phases.

Phase 1: Code repositories and postratification frames (3 months, Oct-Dec 2025)

RB will develop fully-documented and transferable code for end-to-end MRP to be used by SDF data scientists – an early demo is in preparation. RB will then construct postratification frames, containing marginal counts for demographic and area-level combinations (e.g. Hanretty 2019) and assemble area-level context variables known to be discriminating of financial outcomes, for use in Phase 2. Planning on the key methods paper will also start in this phase.

Phase 2: MRP model development and validation (3 months, Jan-Mar 2026)

RB will work with SDF data scientists on modelling the financial outcomes directly within SDF Trusted Research Environment (TRE). This will involve RB visiting SDF offices and supporting data scientists in making decisions around model structure, variable inclusion, and especially incorporating area-level context variables. HASP data scientists will also attend these meetings, to exchange learning on important but low-level decisions around model specification that are rarely documented in statistics primers and tutorials. In March 2026, RB will present the project, and plans for the flagship methods paper, at the AAG conference in San Francisco.

Phase 3: MRP roll-out (3 months, Apr-Jun 2026)

SDF data scientists will generate the key data products and work with SDF Product Manager to on-board datasets to the FINDS data catalogue. This will involve predicting out from the MRP models for the different demographic and area-level subsets under the Economic Wellbeing Explorer dashboard. Each subset will be reported with upper and lower interval ranges. Again, RB will work in an advisory capacity and together with HASP data scientists, will write-up transferable code demonstrations and explanations of the MRP process – the EPB Data:Code paper. The key methods paper will be submitted to AAAG journal.

Phase 4: Communicate (3 months, Jul-Sep 2026)

RB and SDR data scientists will collaborate on building uncertainty visualization encodings into the Economic Wellbeing Explorer dashboard, and with SDF Product Manager and Digital Content and Storytelling Specialist, develop and disseminate data stories that communicate to policy-makers and service providers what the MRP-derived estimates are, how they can be understood and used in decision-making.

Dependencies

FINDS microdata

Work on FINDS individual-level data will be performed within the SDF TRE. All SDF data scientists have approval to work in this environment. RB and HASP data scientists will also require access to the SDF TRE.

SDF are responsible for this dependency and have a clear process in place. Each applicant needs to pass a “Standard Disclosure” or “DBS Check”, attend an online training session covering safe use of the TRE and sign a TRE user agreement.

Census and area-level context data

The project uses Census and area-level context data for generating poststratification frames that, at its maximum level of restriction, requires Safeguarded access. All researchers and analysts included in this proposal already have this level of access.

Any additional area-level context variables collected from other controlled government datasets will be fully open access. The lowest level of aggregation at which these data will be collected is LSOA; it is very unlikely that dependencies on these data will be prohibitive.

Risks

Risk 1. That code libraries for MRP modelling are incompatible with SDF TRE

Mitigation: SDF data scientists have consulted a demo, created as part of this proposal, for end-to-end MRP modelling. We can confirm that the libraries explained in the demo are compatible with the SDF TRE.

Risk 2. FINDS microdata is not suitable for MRP modelling

Mitigation: A key element of MRP is the re-weighting of modelled estimates using a poststratification frame. This typically is defined by cross-sectional demographic characteristics – age, ethnicity, level of education. Although information on FINDS microdata is not attribute-rich in its demographics, containing only age and sex, the neighbourhood (LSOA) in which customers live is consistently recorded. This is an important addition as it means that area-level context variables, which in MRP have been demonstrated to be highly descriminating (Gelman et al. 2016), can be incorporated into the modelling process. We are therefore confident that MRP can be successfully applied to FINDS microdata.

Ethics

Ethical approval for use of data

SDF will complete a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) and seek ethical approval for the entire project through University of Edinburgh.

Data privacy and disclosure

Models using FINDS microdata will be built end-to-end by SDF data scientists within the SDF TRE, supported by RB. Data products – modelled estimates and uncertainty bands – will be published via SDF FINDS data catalogue, and by default have Safeguarded access. As the data products are derived from model probabilities, they are entirely synthetic and cannot be disclosive of individuals.

Responsible communication of uncertainty

Our data stories and visualization tools that explain the modelling process are strategies to ensure responsible use of data. They help users to fully understand the estimates and their limitations, and in this respect support ethical use of smart data.
